# Better helper replies reached the final answer

Our latest small experiment connects a simple harness choice to the final task
result. With the root model unchanged, replies generated by helpers processing
four records at a time led to **18 correct final answers out of 24**, compared
with **12** when the helpers processed 16 at a time. Replies from individual-record
requests led to **17** correct final answers.

This is a promising direction, not a demonstrated general solution. We replayed
saved helper replies on two familiar contexts. The system did not learn to choose
the request size, and this was not a full live deployment comparison.

## What the experiment actually did

The [previous experiment](2026-09-12-helper-size-and-training-exposure.md) generated
helper replies for the same records using three request sizes. Smaller requests
produced more correct local categories overall, but sometimes spoiled an answer.

Here we took those **actual replies**, including their mistakes, and gave them to
the same root model when it requested the categories. We did not replace them
with the correct labels. The root still had to write and run its Python code and
produce the final answer. Each condition covered the same 12 questions, twice,
with paired sampling seeds.

| Source of the saved helper replies | Correct final answers | Wrong final answers | No final answer |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sixteen records per helper request | 12 | 11 | 1 |
| Four records per helper request | 18 | 6 | 0 |
| One record per helper request | 17 | 7 | 0 |

Compared with groups of 16, groups of four changed seven observed wrong answers
to correct answers and changed two correct answers to wrong answers. They also
produced a correct answer on the attempt where the larger-request condition had
no final answer. Thus the six-answer net gain combines five net observed
improvements and one recovered unavailable answer.

The two contexts improved from 4 to 8 and from 8 to 10 correct answers respectively.
Individual-record replies improved only the first context, from 4 to 9; the second
stayed at 8. We cannot conclude that either four or one is generally the best size.

## What stayed fixed—and what did not

The root and helper weights, questions, and request definitions were fixed.
Only the source of the supplied helper map changed intentionally. Each saved map
was reused across six questions, so these are clustered observations, not 24
independent documents per condition.

Equal sampling seeds did not make every root action identical. Before seeing a
helper reply, the root's initial response differed in two of 24 four-versus-sixteen
pairs and six of 24 one-versus-sixteen pairs. This limits a perfectly isolated
causal interpretation. The full report preserves these comparisons rather than
assuming deterministic behavior.

All underlying helper records appeared in its earlier supervised training.
The experiment shows that the way we divide familiar material can matter to the
final answer. It does not establish performance on unseen questions or learned
recursive planning.

## The cost question remains open

The root made 72, 73 and 72 actual model calls in the three conditions. Its input
token totals were 100,719, 102,865 and 100,671 respectively. Root-side work was
therefore similar in this test.

However, the saved helper replies were generated earlier and reused. Their
generation costs must be counted separately. The earlier broader helper panel
found that groups of four used 2.58 times as many total tokens as groups of 16;
individual requests used 8.89 times as many. Those are a different panel's
measurements, not costs to attach directly to this 24-question comparison.
We have not demonstrated end-to-end speed or token savings.

## What we should test next

First, run the smaller-request method with live helper calls on new examples.
Compare it with a fixed large-request baseline and with individual requests,
including all calls, time and tokens. Then ask whether a policy can choose when
to split further or request a fresh check, rather than always paying for smaller
requests.

In parallel, a helper-RL pilot is testing whether local correctness feedback can
improve the helper's weights. Its first attempt saved 128 sampled answers but ran
out of memory before updating. A memory-saving replay is active at this cutoff.
A frozen TREC/news panel excludes the helper's actual supervised-training inputs;
it will check transfer rather than only improvement on familiar material.
